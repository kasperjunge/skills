# Positioning as context

Customers don't evaluate a product in a vacuum. They use cues, such as what it is called, what it is compared to, and who else uses it, to place it in a frame of reference. That frame decides which competitors they compare it to, which features they expect, and what price seems fair. Positioning is the deliberate choice of that frame.

## Why it matters

- **Context sets expectations.** Calling a product a "database" makes customers assume a set of features and competitors. Calling the same product a "spreadsheet" makes them assume a different set.
- **The wrong context hides strengths.** A product whose strengths are irrelevant in its chosen category looks weak, however good it is.
- **Everything downstream depends on it.** Messaging, sales conversations, pricing, onboarding, and the roadmap all assume an answer to "what is this and who is it for". If the answer is unclear, every team invents its own.

## Default positioning

Most products are positioned by accident. The founders chose a category when the product was an idea, and the product changed while the positioning stayed the same. This **default positioning** is rarely chosen; it is inherited. Dunford's process starts by letting go of it.

## Signs that positioning is weak

- Prospects don't understand what the product does, or ask "so it's like X?" with the wrong X.
- Sales cycles are long, and a lot of time goes into explaining before selling.
- Prospects compare the product to competitors the team doesn't consider relevant.
- Win rates are low against some alternatives and high against others, with no clear pattern.
- Marketing reaches people who are not a good fit, so pipeline grows while revenue doesn't.
- Pricing pressure: customers anchor on the price of the wrong category.
- Teams describe the product differently from each other.

## When to reposition

- The product has changed significantly since it was first positioned.
- The market has changed: new competitors, a new category, or a trend that shifts what customers value.
- A new segment loves the product more than the original target does.
- The signs above persist even though the product is good.

Positioning is not permanent, but changing it is expensive. Revisit it deliberately, not every quarter.

## Failure modes

- **Treating positioning as messaging.** Rewording the homepage without changing the frame of reference.
- **Positioning by aspiration.** Describing the product the team wants to have, not the one customers love today.
- **Trying to appeal to everyone.** A broad frame avoids losing anyone and convinces no one.
