# The components

Dunford's positioning has five components and one optional layer. Each is derived from the one before, so work through them in this order.

## 1. Competitive alternatives

What customers would do if your product didn't exist. This is the real competition, and it often isn't another product: a spreadsheet, an intern, an agency, doing nothing, or "the way we've always done it".

- Ask your best customers what they used before and what they would use if you vanished.
- Group alternatives by type, not by brand.
- Include doing nothing. It is often the strongest alternative.

This is the hiring-and-firing view from the `jtbd` skill: the alternatives are what customers hire for the same job.

## 2. Unique attributes

What you have that the alternatives don't. Attributes are capabilities and features, but also delivery model, expertise, business model, or anything else that differs.

- List them relative to the alternatives, not in absolute terms. "Fast" means nothing; "results in seconds instead of a two-day agency turnaround" means something.
- Be factual. An attribute you can't demonstrate isn't one.

## 3. Value (and proof)

What the unique attributes do for customers. Map **attribute → benefit → value**: the feature, what it enables, and what that is worth to the customer.

- Cluster values into two to four **value themes**. Customers remember themes, not lists.
- Each value needs **proof**: a demo, a customer story, a third-party review, a number. Value without proof is a claim.
- Value must be expressed in terms customers care about, such as money, time, risk, or status, not in product vocabulary.

## 4. Best-fit customers

Who cares a lot about that value. Not everyone who could buy, but the customers who buy quickly, don't haggle much, stay, and tell others.

- Describe them by **characteristics** that make them care: a situation, a constraint, a trigger, a size, a team structure. Demographics alone don't help.
- Ask: what does this customer have that makes our value unusually important to them?
- The target should be narrow enough that marketing and sales can find these customers.

In `jtbd` terms, best-fit customers are the ones whose circumstance makes the job urgent and the push of the situation strong.

## 5. Market category

The frame of reference that makes the value obvious to best-fit customers. Naming a category tells customers what to compare you with and what to assume about you. Choose the category whose assumptions put your strengths at the centre. See [Market category](market-category.md).

## 6. Relevant trend (optional)

A trend that makes your value more urgent right now. Use it carefully:

- Only use a trend your best-fit customers already care about and that is connected to your value.
- A trend is a layer on top of the positioning, not the positioning itself. Swapping the trend should not change the other components.
- A trend without a connection to your value makes you look like you are chasing hype.

## Failure modes

- **Starting with the category.** Picking the category first and backfilling the rest from it.
- **Attributes without alternatives.** Listing features without saying what they are better than.
- **Value without proof.**
- **Best-fit customers described as everyone.**
