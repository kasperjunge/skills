# Organizing around jobs

Christensen argues that understanding a job is only useful if the product, the experiences around it, and the organization are built to do that job. Many companies understand their customers' jobs early, then drift toward optimizing products, metrics, and internal processes instead.

## Products

- **Design to the job, not the category.** Requirements come from the job's functional, emotional, and social [dimensions](dimensions.md), not from what competitors offer.
- **Say what not to build.** A job makes it clear which features don't help the progress, even if customers or competitors ask for them.
- **Integrate around the job.** Do everything the job needs, including steps outside the core product (setup, support, financing, onboarding), when those steps are where people struggle.

## Experiences

People hire the whole experience of finding, buying, and using a solution. Each step should remove [anxiety and habit](forces-of-progress.md) and make the progress visible early. Christensen's example is Southern New Hampshire University: it built its online program around adult learners trying to finish a degree alongside work and family. It changed how it answered enquiries (calling back within minutes), helped with financial aid, and assigned advisers who stayed in touch, because those were the points where adult learners struggled.

## Purpose brands

A brand that becomes synonymous with a job is a **purpose brand**. When the job arises, people think of that brand first. This only works if the product consistently does the job; a purpose brand is earned through the experience, not created by advertising.

## Metrics

Traditional metrics (revenue, units sold, satisfaction scores) measure the business, not the job. They can look healthy while the product slowly stops doing the job. Measure job progress where possible: do people actually make the progress they hired the product for, and do they keep hiring it (the little hire)?

## Common drift

Christensen names three traps that pull organizations away from the job:

- **Fallacy of active vs. passive data.** Early on, a company actively seeks out data about the job. As it grows, data arrives passively from its own operations (sales, usage, customer attributes) and gets shaped around the product, so the job fades from view.
- **Fallacy of surface growth.** Growing by selling more products to existing customers, or adding adjacent offerings, instead of doing the job better. The core job gets neglected.
- **Fallacy of conforming data.** Collecting and trusting data that confirms what the organization already believes, because it is easy to measure, while the job and its circumstances aren't captured in any dataset.

## Failure modes

- **Insight without change.** Jobs are documented but don't change what is built, sold, or measured.
- **Feature creep.** Adding what customers ask for even when it doesn't serve the job.
- **Department silos.** No team owns the whole job, so the struggles between product, sales, and support go unfixed.
