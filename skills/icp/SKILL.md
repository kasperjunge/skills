# Ideal Customer Profile

An ICP describes the customers for whom a specific offering creates
substantial value and whom the business can acquire, serve, and retain
on viable terms. It should help decide where to focus and whom to
deprioritize.

Treat the ICP as a testable hypothesis. Its specificity should reflect
the available evidence.

## Distinguish the decisions

Keep these related concepts separate:

- **ICP:** Which customers or accounts are a strong fit?
- **Buyer roles:** Who uses, champions, approves, buys, or blocks?
- **Buying readiness:** Why might this customer act now?
- **Market size:** How many potential customers meet the criteria?

In B2B, the account is usually the unit of analysis. In consumer
businesses, use the individual or household as appropriate.

A customer can fit the ICP without being ready to buy. An eager buyer
can still be a poor fit.

## Start with customer value

Establish the offering, the customer problem, and the alternatives
customers use today, including manual work and doing nothing.

Identify the conditions under which the offering creates meaningfully
better outcomes than those alternatives. Ask what makes the problem
more consequential and the product's advantages more valuable for
some customers than for others.

Use industry, company size, geography, or technology only when they
help explain fit or provide a useful way to find likely matches.
Do not invent narrow demographic or firmographic boundaries to make
a profile appear precise.

## Learn from contrasting cases

When evidence is available, compare customers who:

- Adopt, achieve results, renew, or expand.
- Buy but struggle to adopt or require disproportionate support.
- Churn, choose an alternative, or decide not to buy.

Look for differences in needs, workflows, constraints, alternatives,
and ability to realize value.

Consider both sides of fit:

- **Customer outcomes:** Problem severity, value achieved, time to
  value, adoption, and sustained use.
- **Business outcomes:** Acquisition effort, sales cycle, revenue,
  delivery cost, support burden, retention, and expansion.

Do not equate the largest contract, most enthusiastic interview,
or easiest sale with the best customer.

Separate observed facts, interpretations, and untested assumptions.
When data is sparse, produce a provisional profile and identify
the evidence needed to improve it.

## Turn patterns into usable criteria

For each proposed criterion, explain:

- Why it predicts customer value or business viability.
- How someone could observe or verify it.
- What evidence supports it.
- Whether it is required, preferred, or disqualifying.

Separate underlying fit from observable proxies and timing signals.
For example, a hiring announcement may suggest a growing coordination
problem; it does not establish that the problem exists.

Use hard exclusions for conditions that prevent success or make the
relationship unviable. Treat weaker indicators as preferences.
Missing information is unknown, not an automatic failure.

Avoid weighted scores and precise thresholds unless evidence or an
explicit operating need justifies them.

## Choose a focus

When several segments look promising, compare them on customer value,
ability to adopt, accessibility, and business viability.

Recommend a primary segment when the evidence supports a choice.
Explain the tradeoffs and which plausible segments are being
deprioritized.

Keep materially different needs or buying conditions in separate
profiles. Do not combine incompatible segments into a broad profile
that describes almost everyone.

## Record the profile

Adapt the detail to the request. A useful ICP record contains:

- **Scope:** Offering, market, and unit of analysis.
- **Profile:** A short description of the best-fit customer and why.
- **Problem and alternatives:** What they need to accomplish and
  how they handle it today.
- **Fit criteria:** Required conditions, preferences, exclusions,
  observable signals, and supporting evidence.
- **Buying context:** Relevant roles and triggers, kept separate
  from enduring fit.
- **Uncertainty:** Assumptions, counterexamples, and evidence gaps.
- **Validation:** What to investigate next and what findings would
  cause the profile to change.

For a review, prioritize unsupported criteria, contradictions,
and changes that would alter targeting decisions.

## Refine through use

Test whether the profile distinguishes customers who succeed from
those who do not. Where possible, test it on cases beyond those used
to define it.

Track outcomes by segment, including adoption, retention, and cost
to serve—not just response rates or closed deals.

Revise the profile when evidence changes. Check whether poor outcomes
reflect customer fit, product limitations, or execution before
excluding a segment.
