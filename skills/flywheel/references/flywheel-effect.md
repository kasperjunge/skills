# The flywheel effect

Collins describes a huge, heavy flywheel. Pushing it takes great effort and it barely moves at first. Keep pushing in the same direction and it turns faster: two turns, four, eight, a hundred, a thousand. At some point momentum takes over and it seems to turn almost on its own. Looking back, there was no single push that made the difference. It was all of them, in a consistent direction.

## Principles

- **No miracle moment.** Good-to-great transformations never happen in one fell swoop: no single defining action, grand program, killer innovation, or lucky break.
- **Cumulative.** Each turn builds on the work done before. Results compound.
- **Consistent direction.** Momentum comes from pushing the same way, turn after turn, over years.
- **Momentum attracts support.** Visible results get people to align with the flywheel on their own. Results come first; buy-in follows, rather than the other way round.
- **From outside it looks like a breakthrough.** Observers see a sudden leap; insiders see a long, organic process.

## The doom loop

The opposite of the flywheel. Instead of building momentum, the organization:

1. Seeks a dramatic breakthrough: a new program, leader, acquisition, or fad.
2. Gets disappointing results.
3. Reacts without understanding why, and changes direction.
4. Repeats, with a new program, never building momentum.

| Flywheel | Doom loop |
|---|---|
| Builds on accumulated momentum. | Starts over with each new direction. |
| Results produce buy-in. | Tries to motivate with launches and announcements. |
| Steady, consistent pushes. | Lurching reactions. |
| Grounded in understanding. | Chases fads and quick fixes. |

## Questions

- What has the organization consistently pushed on for years, and what did it produce?
- How many "transformational" programs have started and been abandoned?
- Are results coming from accumulated momentum or from one-off efforts?
