# The goal and its measures

Before improving a system, say what it is for. Goldratt's first question is "what is the goal?" For a business, the goal is to make more money now and in the future. Other systems have other goals, but every system needs one, because a constraint is defined by what limits progress toward it.

## Throughput accounting

Goldratt replaces cost-based measures with three that relate directly to the goal:

- **Throughput (T):** the rate at which the system generates money through sales. Sales revenue minus truly variable costs, such as raw materials. Products made but not sold are not throughput.
- **Inventory or investment (I):** money tied up in things the system intends to sell, or in the things it uses to make them.
- **Operating expense (OE):** money spent turning inventory into throughput, including wages.

From these: net profit = T − OE, and return on investment = (T − OE) / I.

Of the three, only throughput has no upper limit. Cutting operating expense and inventory helps, but has a floor. Most improvement opportunities come from raising throughput.

## Why local measures mislead

Traditional measures judge each part of a system on its own: machine utilisation, cost per unit, people being busy. Goldratt's point is that a system of local optima is not an optimal system.

- **Busy is not productive.** A non-constraint working at full capacity just builds inventory in front of the constraint.
- **Cost per unit hides the constraint.** Saving an hour at a non-constraint reduces nothing; the system still runs at the constraint's pace.
- **Balanced capacity is a trap.** If every step has the same capacity, normal variation makes the whole system slower than any single step. See below.

## Dependent events and statistical fluctuations

In *The Goal*, Goldratt illustrates this with a hike, where the slowest scout (Herbie) sets the pace of the troop, and a dice game, where each player passes matches to the next. When steps depend on each other and each step's output varies, delays accumulate down the line while gains are lost waiting for slower steps. The result: the system as a whole is slower than its average step suggests, and it gets worse with more steps and more variation.

The fix is not to make every step equally fast. It is to accept that one step is the constraint, protect it with a buffer, and keep spare capacity everywhere else.

## Failure modes

- **No stated goal.** Improvement efforts pull in different directions.
- **Measuring parts, not the whole.** Utilisation and efficiency targets for every team or machine.
- **Cost cutting as the only lever.** Ignoring throughput, where most of the opportunity is.
