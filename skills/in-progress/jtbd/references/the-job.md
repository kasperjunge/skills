# The job

Christensen defines a job as **the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance**. When a product helps with that progress, the person "hires" it. When it stops helping, or something better comes along, they "fire" it.

## The three parts

- **Progress**: movement from a current state toward a better one. A job is a process, not a single moment, and it describes change the person wants in their life or work.
- **Circumstance**: the context in which the job arises: where, when, with whom, under what constraints, and what came before. The circumstance is what makes a job a job. The same person hires different products in different circumstances.
- **Struggle**: the job becomes visible when something gets in the way of progress. Moesta calls these **struggling moments**. Without a struggle, people don't look for something new.

## The milkshake

Christensen's best-known example. A fast-food chain tried to sell more milkshakes by improving them along the dimensions customers named in surveys (thicker, more chocolatey, cheaper). Sales didn't move. Watching who bought milkshakes, and when, showed that many were bought early in the morning by people alone, on their way to work. They hired the milkshake to make a long, boring commute more interesting and to stay full until lunch. It could be held in one hand, took a long time to finish through a thin straw, and wasn't messy. Its competitors were bagels, bananas, doughnuts, and boredom, not other milkshakes. In the afternoon, parents hired the same milkshake for a different job: a treat that made them feel like good parents. One product, two circumstances, two jobs, two sets of requirements.

## What a job is not

| Not a job | Why | Closer to a job |
|---|---|---|
| A customer segment or persona ("millennial professionals") | Describes who, not what progress they seek. Correlated attributes don't cause purchases. | "Feel in control of money when the first real salary arrives." |
| A feature ("export to PDF") | Describes the solution, not the progress. | "Send a polished report the client will trust, without reformatting." |
| A task ("click send") | A step, with no reason or circumstance. | "Get sign-off before Friday so the launch isn't delayed." |
| A broad need ("communicate") | Too abstract to act on; lacks circumstance. | "Keep a distributed team in sync across time zones without daily calls." |

## Writing a job statement

There is no strict format in this school, but a useful shape names the circumstance, the progress, and the outcome:

> When [circumstance], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome / better state].

Example: "When I'm commuting alone early in the morning, I want something to occupy me and keep me full, so I arrive at work not hungry and not bored."

Good job statements:

- name a specific circumstance, not a demographic;
- describe progress, not a product or feature;
- are solution-agnostic: several very different products could be hired for them;
- include why the progress matters (see [dimensions](dimensions.md));
- are grounded in observed behavior, not in what people say they want.

## Stability

Jobs change slowly. People have been hiring things to stay entertained on a commute for a long time; the solutions (radio, podcasts, milkshakes) change. This is why a job is a better anchor for a product than a feature set or a technology.

## Failure modes

- **Solution in disguise.** The job names the product ("I want to use a CRM").
- **No circumstance.** The job could apply to anyone, anytime, so it guides nothing.
- **Demographic thinking.** Grouping customers by attributes instead of by circumstance and progress.
- **Too big or too small.** "Be happy" is too abstract; "click the save button" is a task. Aim for the level at which a person would choose between solutions.
- **Invented jobs.** Jobs written from intuition, never checked against what people actually did.
