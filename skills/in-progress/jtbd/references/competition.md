# Hiring, firing, and competition

If people hire products to do jobs, then a product's competition is everything else that could be hired for the same job, and every hire means something else gets fired.

## Competition is defined by the job

Competitors are not the products in the same category. They are whatever people currently hire to make the same progress. The milkshake competed with bagels, bananas, and boredom. A project management tool competes with spreadsheets, email, a weekly meeting, and a manager who keeps it all in their head.

This changes what "winning" means: it means doing the job better than the alternatives people actually use, not having more features than the category leader.

## Nonconsumption

Often the biggest competitor is **nonconsumption**: people who have the job but hire nothing, because every available solution is too expensive, too complicated, or inaccessible. They make do, work around it, or give up. Nonconsumption is a large source of growth, because these people aren't loyal to any alternative. Christensen's work on disruption is largely about products that first serve nonconsumers with a simpler, cheaper, good-enough solution.

## Firing

To hire something new, people must fire something old, even if the "old" is a workaround or doing nothing. Firing has costs: switching effort, lost investment, habits, and the risk the new thing is worse. Understanding what gets fired, and why, is as important as understanding what gets hired. See [forces of progress](forces-of-progress.md).

## Big hire and little hire

Christensen separates two moments:

- **Big hire**: the moment of purchase or sign-up.
- **Little hire**: each time the product is actually used to do the job.

Many products win the big hire and lose the little hire: bought but not used, signed up for and abandoned. Sales numbers only show the big hire. Usage and retention show whether the product is really doing the job.

## Questions that reveal the competition

- Before this product, how did people get the job done? What did they do instead?
- What did they stop using or doing when they started?
- Who has the job but does nothing about it? What stops them?
- When people stop using the product, what do they go back to?

## Failure modes

- **Category blindness.** Only tracking same-category competitors and missing the workaround that most people actually use.
- **Ignoring nonconsumption.** Fighting over existing customers while a larger group goes unserved.
- **Counting only the big hire.** Treating purchases as success when the product isn't being used.
