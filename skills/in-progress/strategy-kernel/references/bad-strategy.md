# Bad strategy

Bad strategy isn't simply the absence of good strategy. It is an active substitute that looks like strategy but skips the hard work of diagnosis, choice, and coordination. Use it to review an existing strategy or test a draft.

## The four hallmarks

### Fluff

Words that sound strategic but say nothing: buzzwords, jargon, and inflated abstractions. "Our fundamental strategy is one of customer-centric intermediation" means "we are a bank." Fluff creates the illusion of high-level thinking.

*Test:* Restate it in plain words. If little is left, it's fluff.

### Failure to face the challenge

The strategy never names the obstacle. Without a defined challenge, you can't judge whether the strategy is any good, and nobody can tell whether it is working.

*Test:* Ask "What problem does this solve?" If there is no clear answer, there is no diagnosis.

### Mistaking goals for strategy

Statements of desire ("grow 20% a year", "become number one") presented as strategy. Goals tell people what to want, not how to overcome what stands in the way.

*Test:* Ask "How?" If the answer is another goal, there is no guiding policy.

### Bad strategic objectives

Objectives that fail in one of two ways:

- **Dog's dinner**: a long list of things to do, with no focus and no connection. Often the result of collecting everyone's priorities.
- **Blue sky**: objectives that restate the challenge or require something nobody knows how to do.

*Test:* Count the objectives, and check whether each one is feasible and connected to the crux.

## Why bad strategy happens

- **Unwillingness to choose.** Choosing means saying no to people, units, and ideas. It is easier to include everyone.
- **Template-style strategy.** Filling in vision, mission, values, and goals and calling it strategy.
- **Positive thinking.** The belief that vision and commitment are enough, without a hard look at obstacles.

## Review checklist

Run through these questions for the full kernel:

1. **Diagnosis**: Does it name a specific challenge and the crux? Is it honest?
2. **Guiding policy**: Does it address the crux? Does it rule out attractive options? What is the source of advantage?
3. **Coherent actions**: Do they follow from the policy and reinforce each other? Are they concrete, owned, and resourced?
4. **Proximate objectives**: Are they reachable and relevant?
5. **Fluff**: Is every sentence plain and specific?
6. **Assumptions**: What must be true? What signal would show the strategy is wrong?

## Fixing it

Name the hallmark, point to the offending part, and rewrite it: replace fluff with plain claims, goals with a policy that says how, and lists with the few actions that reinforce each other. Bad strategy is common and usually comes from good intentions; the fix is sharpening, not starting over.
