# Guiding policy

The guiding policy is the overall approach to the challenge named in the diagnosis. It is not a goal, a vision, or a list of actions. It works like a guardrail: it directs and constrains action without spelling out every step.

## What a guiding policy does

- **It responds to the diagnosis.** If it's unclear how the policy addresses the crux, one of the two is wrong.
- **It rules things out.** A policy that excludes nothing is not a policy. The real test is whether it rules out options that are attractive, not just ones nobody wanted.
- **It creates or uses advantage.** It says why this approach should work for this actor, drawing on a [source of power](sources-of-power.md) such as leverage, focus, or an asymmetry with competitors.
- **It reduces complexity.** People across the organization can use it to make decisions without asking permission each time.

## Policy vs. goal

| Goal (not a policy) | Guiding policy |
|---|---|
| Become the market leader. | Win the segment incumbents underserve: small clinics that need setup in a day. |
| Improve customer satisfaction. | Make one support team own each customer end to end, instead of routing tickets. |
| Be the most innovative company. | Bet on one platform shift early and accept losing the current product line. |

A goal says where you want to be. A policy says how you will get there, given the obstacle.

## Kinds of policy

Policies that differ in kind, not just degree:

- **Concentrate**: put resources on one segment, product, or problem where they can be decisive.
- **Go around**: avoid the opponent's strength and attack where they are weak or can't respond.
- **Change the game**: shift the basis of competition to one where the actor has an edge.
- **Ride a shift**: use a change in technology, regulation, or behavior before others adapt.
- **Fix the weakest link**: if performance is limited by one bottleneck, make that the whole policy.
- **Wait and prepare**: when the crux isn't addressable yet, build options and position for when it is.

Each candidate policy can be judged by what it relies on, what it rules out, what it costs, and what must be true for it to work.

## Example

Apple in 1997. **Diagnosis**: Apple was near bankruptcy, with a sprawling, confusing product line and no product good enough to win. **Guiding policy**: cut to a small number of excellent products and focus the whole company on making them great. The policy ruled out dozens of existing products, licensing clones, and competing on price.

## Failure modes

- **Goal in disguise.** "Our policy is to grow." By doing what, differently?
- **Everything policy.** Tries to serve every customer and every priority, and rules out nothing.
- **Disconnected from diagnosis.** A good idea that doesn't address the crux.
- **No source of advantage.** It would work equally well (or badly) for anyone.
- **Unaffordable.** It needs resources the actor doesn't have and can't get.
