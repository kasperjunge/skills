# Coherent actions

Coherent actions are the specific, coordinated steps that carry out the guiding policy. Rumelt insists that strategy is about action: a diagnosis and a policy without actions are just commentary.

## What makes actions coherent

- **They follow from the policy.** Each action exists because the policy calls for it. If an action would happen anyway, it isn't part of the strategy.
- **They reinforce each other.** Action A makes action B more effective, and B makes A more effective. The combination does more than the parts.
- **They are coordinated.** Timing, ownership, and resources line up. Coordination is often the main value a strategy adds, because it produces effects that independent units can't produce alone.
- **They commit resources.** Real actions move money, people, and attention. They displace other work.
- **They are concrete.** Someone can start them. "Improve quality" is not an action. "Put every new release through a one-week beta with ten named customers" is.

## Coherence vs. a wish list

A common failure is a list of initiatives, each sensible on its own, that don't connect. They compete for the same resources and pull in different directions. Signs of a wish list:

- Each action belongs to a different department and was proposed by that department.
- Removing one action wouldn't change how the others work.
- The list is long (more than 3–5 main actions).
- Nothing on the list stops or replaces existing work.

## Example

Operation Desert Storm (1991). **Diagnosis**: Iraq expected a frontal attack on its fortified positions in Kuwait. **Guiding policy**: avoid the strong front and attack around it. **Coherent actions**: an air campaign to blind Iraqi command; deception that kept attention on the coast and the front; and a large, secret "left hook" through the western desert. Each action made the others work: the deception only mattered because of the hook, and the hook was only possible because the air campaign removed Iraq's ability to see it.

## Deriving actions from a policy

1. Ask "What must be true for this policy to succeed?" Each answer suggests an action.
2. For each action, identify its owner, its cost, and what it displaces.
3. For every pair of actions, decide whether they reinforce, conflict, or are independent. Cut or change actions that conflict.
4. Sequence them: which action must come first, and which depend on earlier results.
5. Give each action a [proximate objective](proximate-objectives.md): a near-term result that shows it is working.

## Failure modes

- **Wish list.** Unconnected initiatives that don't reinforce each other.
- **Too many actions.** Resources spread thin across more than the 3–5 that matter most.
- **No owner or no resources.** An action nobody is accountable for won't happen.
- **Business as usual.** Actions that are already happening, relabeled as strategy.
- **Contradictions.** One action undermines another, e.g. cutting costs while promising premium service.
