# Diagnosis

The diagnosis explains what is going on. It takes a situation that is complex, ambiguous, and full of facts, and simplifies it by naming the few aspects that matter most. Rumelt calls this the most important part of the kernel: a strategy can be no better than its diagnosis.

## What a diagnosis does

- **It simplifies.** Reality has too many details to act on. The diagnosis picks what matters and pushes aside what doesn't.
- **It names a challenge.** It says what stands in the way, not only what is wanted. "We want to double revenue" is a goal. "Customers churn after month three because onboarding never gets them to first value" is a diagnosis.
- **It is a judgment, not a fact.** Different diagnoses of the same situation are possible and lead to different strategies. A diagnosis can't be proven, only made more or less convincing by evidence.
- **It points at action.** A good diagnosis makes some actions look obviously relevant and others obviously irrelevant.

## The crux

In *The Crux*, Rumelt adds that the diagnosis should find the **crux**: the most important part of the challenge that can actually be addressed. The crux has two properties:

1. **Important**: solving it unlocks progress on much of the rest.
2. **Addressable**: there is a plausible way to make progress on it with the resources available.

A challenge that is important but impossible is not the crux. Neither is one that is easy but changes nothing. Finding the crux often means breaking a large challenge into parts and asking, for each, "How much would solving this help?" and "Can it actually be solved?"

## Framing

A diagnosis often works through a frame: an analogy, a model, or a reclassification that makes the situation understandable. Examples:

- "This isn't a sales problem, it's a positioning problem."
- "We are a commodity supplier pretending to be a premium brand."
- "Our competitor has to defend a large installed base; we don't."

Different frames of the same facts suggest different strategies. The best frame explains the most facts and suggests the clearest action.

## Questions that surface a diagnosis

- What has changed recently, in the market, technology, regulation, team, or customers?
- What has already been tried, and why did it fail or stall?
- What is the thing everyone knows but nobody says?
- If only one thing could be fixed, what would it be?
- Who or what works against the actor? What do they want, and what limits them?
- What does the actor have that others don't, and the reverse?

## Failure modes

- **No diagnosis.** Jumping straight to goals or actions.
- **Restated goal.** "The challenge is to grow faster." Keep asking "What prevents it?" until an obstacle appears.
- **Laundry list.** Ten challenges with equal weight, and no sense of which one, if solved, would make the others easier.
- **Blame without a lever.** "The market is bad." The diagnosis must point to something the actor can act on.
- **Comfortable diagnosis.** One that avoids hard truths about the actor's own organization or product.

## Testing a diagnosis

- Can it be stated in two or three sentences?
- Does it explain the known facts, including the awkward ones?
- Does it name an obstacle, not a desire?
- Would a well-informed outsider recognize it as true, even if uncomfortable?
- Does it make some actions clearly more relevant than others?
