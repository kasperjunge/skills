# Sources of power

A guiding policy works because it draws on some source of advantage. Rumelt describes several recurring sources of power. Use them to generate policy options and to check that a chosen policy explains *why* it should work for this actor.

## Leverage

Concentrating effort on a pivotal point so a modest action produces a large effect. Leverage comes from anticipating what others will do, from finding a pivot point (a bottleneck, a key customer, a standard), and from concentrating resources instead of spreading them.

*Ask:* Where would a small push create a large result?

## Proximate objectives

Setting targets close enough to reach, turning ambiguity into a solvable problem. See [proximate objectives](proximate-objectives.md).

*Ask:* What is the nearest target that would make real progress on the crux?

## Chain-link systems

When performance depends on several links working together, the system is only as strong as its weakest link. Improving any other link does nothing. This explains why organizations get stuck: no single unit can raise quality alone. It also means a rival's excellent chain-link system is hard to copy.

*Ask:* Which link limits the whole system? Who has the authority to fix all the links together?

## Design

Strategy as the deliberate fitting together of parts into a whole, where each part supports the others. Tightly designed strategies are powerful but demand resources and coordination, and they are fragile if the situation changes.

*Ask:* How do the pieces fit? What would break if one were removed?

## Focus

Concentrating on a segment or need where the actor can be much better than anyone serving the broader market. Focus usually means designing a whole system around one type of customer.

*Ask:* Which customers or problems could be served far better than by anyone else, if the rest were ignored?

## Growth

Rumelt warns that growth is an outcome of advantage, not a strategy. Buying growth (through acquisitions, discounts, or expansion) without an underlying advantage destroys value. Healthy growth comes from increasing demand for a superior capability.

*Ask:* Is this growth driven by an advantage, or an attempt to buy one?

## Using advantage

An advantage is an asymmetry that lets an actor produce more value or capture more of it than rivals. Advantages are worth more when they are hard to copy, when demand for what they produce is growing, and when they are deepened rather than just exploited. Ways to increase the value of an advantage: deepen it, broaden its use, create higher demand for it, and strengthen the barriers to copying it.

*Ask:* What does the actor have that rivals can't easily get? How can it be deepened?

## Dynamics

Waves of change (in technology, regulation, cost structure, or customer behavior) upset established positions and create openings. The power comes from seeing the shift early and positioning for where the new equilibrium will settle.

*Ask:* What is changing, and who does the change favor? What will the landscape look like once it settles?

## Inertia and entropy

Organizations resist change (inertia) and drift toward disorder without active management (entropy). Both are weaknesses in a rival you can exploit, and dangers inside the actor's own organization that the strategy must account for.

*Ask:* Which competitors are too slow to respond? Where is the actor's own organization stuck or decaying?

## Using them

A strong guiding policy usually rests on one or two clear sources of power. If none apply, the policy probably has no advantage behind it.
